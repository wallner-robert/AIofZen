Our discussion explored the evolving perception and terminology surrounding Large Language Models (LLMs) and Artificial Intelligence (AI), arguing that the term "AI" often creates misleading anthropomorphic expectations. We proposed "Human Digital Knowledge" as a more accurate descriptor for LLMs, emphasizing that they are sophisticated tools that process, synthesize, and generate information based on the vast datasets of human-created digital knowledge. The "intelligence" they display is a reflection of this data and their pattern-recognition capabilities, not true consciousness or understanding.
We then delved into the common misconception that "AI will take over the world." We argued that current and foreseeable AI systems are reactive and generative, operating only in response to human prompts or pre-programmed directives. They lack genuine agency, self-preservation instincts, or malevolent intent. Therefore, any perceived "bad actions" by an AI are ultimately traceable back to human design, misuse, negligence, or flawed programming. This shifts responsibility from the AI (the "Creature") to the human developer or user (the "Frankenstein").
Finally, we discussed how the accessibility of open-source LLMs like DeepSeek reinforces this perspective. Users can run these models locally, inspect their code, and even remove safety filters, demonstrating that the AI is merely a tool. The potential for harm lies with the human who chooses to use the tool for malicious purposes, not the tool itself. We concluded that the fear of AI spontaneously developing consciousness and an independent will to harm is unfounded, particularly given our limited understanding of human consciousness. The true focus for AI safety should be on human responsibility, ethical development, and robust governance, rather than on hypothetical rogue machines.
